Salmorejo

Salmorejo originates in Córdoba, in the heart of Andalusia, where winters are mild but summers reach extreme temperatures, and a cold, thick, filling soup was the ideal solution for field workers who needed energy without lighting a stove. The name comes from "salmuera," referring probably to old salting practices, though the modern, tomato-based recipe only emerged once tomatoes brought from the Americas became a Spanish staple, in the 19th century.\n\nUnlike the thinner gazpacho, which combines several vegetables, salmorejo keeps a minimalist ingredient list and relies on a high proportion of bread to reach a thick, near-cream consistency. The garnish of hard-boiled egg and jamón, usually added right before serving, turns the dish from a simple cold soup into a complete meal, which is why in Córdoba it is often served as a main course at lunch rather than just a starter.

🍞

Far more bread than gazpacho

Salmorejo uses roughly three times more bread relative to tomatoes than gazpacho, which is what makes it thick as a cream rather than a soup.

🥄

Eaten with a spoon, not drunk

Unlike gazpacho, which can be sipped from a glass, salmorejo is dense enough to sit on a spoon, almost like a savory pâté.

🥚

Chopped hard-boiled egg and jamón on top

The traditional garnish — crumbled hard-boiled egg and diced jamón ibérico — adds texture and salt to the smooth tomato cream.

🫒

Just tomato, bread, garlic and oil

No cucumber, no pepper — salmorejo relies on only four core ingredients, emulsified until velvety.

Ingredients 4 servings

  • 1 kg very ripe tomatoes, quartered
  • 250g day-old white bread, crusts removed
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 150 ml extra-virgin olive oil
  • 1 tbsp sherry vinegar
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 2 hard-boiled eggs, for garnish
  • 80g jamón ibérico, diced, for garnish
  • Olive oil, for serving

How to make it

  1. 1Put the stale bread pieces in a bowl, cover them with cold water and let them soak for 10 minutes, then squeeze out the excess water well.
  2. 2Put the tomatoes, garlic and soaked bread into a blender and blitz on high speed until you get a fine paste.
  3. 3With the blender still running, pour in the olive oil in a thin, steady stream, as you would for mayonnaise, until the mixture turns smooth and emulsified.
  4. 4Add the sherry vinegar and salt, then blend for another minute, until the mixture is completely velvety.
  5. 5Pass the cream through a fine sieve to remove any seeds or skins, if you want a perfectly smooth texture.
  6. 6Cover and refrigerate for at least 2 hours, since salmorejo is always served very cold.
  7. 7Ladle into bowls, sprinkle with crumbled hard-boiled egg and diced jamón ibérico, then drizzle with a thread of olive oil just before serving.
