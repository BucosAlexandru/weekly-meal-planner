Banga Soup

Banga Soup — also known locally as 'ofe akwu' — is the defining soup of Nigeria's Delta peoples, particularly the Urhobo, Itsekiri, and Ijaw communities, where oil palms grow abundantly along waterways and mangroves. Unlike many other Nigerian soups that use industrially processed palm oil, Banga stays true to the traditional technique of hand-extracting the pulp directly from fresh fruits. The distinctive, slightly bitter flavors of uziza bark and beletete leaves aren't easily found outside the region, which is why authentic Banga remains difficult to perfectly replicate away from the source. The soup is closely tied to the cultural identity of Delta communities, often served at traditional ceremonies and major community events, where it's prepared in large, communal pots for hundreds of guests at once.

🌿

Banga spice blend

A specific blend of uziza bark, beletete leaves, and alligator pepper gives the soup its bitter, slightly spicy aroma, impossible to replicate without these ingredients.

🍚

Served with cassava starch

Traditionally accompanied by starch (fermented cassava swallow) or pounded yam, eaten by hand and dipped directly into the soup.

Ingredients 4 servings

  • 800 g fresh palm fruits (or 400 g concentrated palm fruit pulp)
  • 2 L water
  • 300 g fresh fish, cut into pieces
  • 150 g deboned smoked fish
  • 2 tbsp ground uziza bark
  • 2 tbsp dried beletete leaves
  • 1 tsp ground alligator pepper
  • 2 scotch bonnet chilies
  • Salt to taste

How to make it

  1. 1Fresh palm fruits boil in water for thirty minutes, until the skin softens and the pulp becomes easy to crush.
  2. 2The boiled fruits are hand-crushed in a mortar or pounded, extracting the oily pulp and separating it from the seeds and skin.
  3. 3The palm fruit paste is mixed with warm water and strained through a fine sieve, producing a thick, orange liquid that forms the base of the soup.
  4. 4The strained liquid boils in a large pot over medium heat for twenty minutes, until the oil begins to separate slightly on the surface.
  5. 5The ground uziza bark, beletete leaves, and alligator pepper are added to the soup, stirring well to distribute the flavors evenly.
  6. 6The deboned smoked fish, broken into pieces, goes into the soup and simmers for ten minutes, letting the flavor infuse the liquid.
  7. 7Whole chilies are added for heat, and the soup continues simmering on low heat for another ten minutes.
  8. 8The fresh fish pieces are added in the final five minutes, cooking gently so they don't fall apart, and the soup is seasoned with salt and served hot alongside starch or pounded yam.
