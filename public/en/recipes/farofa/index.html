Farofa

Farofa predates the arrival of the Portuguese in Brazil, rooted in the indigenous Tupi practice of roasting manioc flour over a fire, a technique that turned a starchy root into a durable, portable staple. Portuguese colonizers adopted it quickly, and enslaved West Africans brought their own toasted-grain traditions that blended into the same dish, so that farofa's basic method — flour toasted in fat until dry and golden — carries three culinary lineages within one simple technique. Today farofa rarely appears alone; it is the accompaniment brought to the table alongside feijoada, churrasco and countless other Brazilian mains, valued for the crunch and textural contrast it brings to soft beans, saucy stews and grilled meats. Regional and family versions multiply endlessly, from the bacon-and-egg style common in Rio to versions studded with banana, raisins or sausage further inland, but the toasted flour base remains the one constant across the entire country.

Butter toasts the flour, grain by grain

Cassava flour is stirred constantly over low heat until every grain is coated in fat and lightly golden — the technique that defines proper farofa.

🥓

Bacon and egg add richness

Crisped bacon and scrambled egg curds turn a plain toasted flour into a savoury, almost stuffing-like side.

🍚

The essential feijoada partner

Farofa is sprinkled over almost every plate of feijoada in Brazil, adding crunch against the soft beans and rice.

🌰

Endless home variations

Bananas, raisins, sausage or hard-boiled egg all turn up in regional farofa recipes passed down within families.

Ingredients 4 servings

  • 500g cassava (manioc) flour
  • 150g butter
  • 1 onion, finely chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 100g smoked bacon, diced
  • 2 eggs, beaten
  • 1 bunch green onions, chopped
  • 50g pitted black olives, chopped
  • Salt and pepper to taste

How to make it

  1. 1Melt the butter in a large skillet over medium heat and add the diced bacon, frying until crisp and golden.
  2. 2Add the chopped onion and garlic and cook for five minutes until softened and fragrant.
  3. 3Pour in the beaten eggs and scramble them quickly into small curds among the onion and bacon.
  4. 4Reduce the heat to low and sprinkle in the cassava flour gradually, stirring constantly with a wooden spoon so it toasts evenly rather than clumping.
  5. 5Keep stirring for eight to ten minutes, until every grain is coated in fat and turns a light golden colour.
  6. 6Fold in the chopped green onions and black olives during the last two minutes of cooking.
  7. 7Season with salt and pepper and serve warm, spooned generously over feijoada, rice or grilled meats.
